c. 700 BC
Hesíodo
Works and Days · Erga kai Hēmerai
+
Context: A didactic poem addressed to his brother Perses. Contains the most extensive and detailed account of the Pandora myth, the Golden Age, and the pithos episode. The canonical source for the myth.

“First of all the deathless gods who dwell on Olympus made a golden race of mortal men who lived in the time of Cronos when he was reigning in heaven. And they lived like gods without sorrow of heart, remote and free from toil and grief.”

— Works and Days, vv. 109–116 · Hesiod, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914) · Source of Lucas Cranach’s painting

“And the Father of men and gods commanded the famous Lame One to make haste and mix earth with water and to put in it the voice and strength of human kind, and fashion a sweet, lovely maiden-shape, like to the immortal goddesses in face.”

— Works and Days, vv. 59–63 · Hesiod, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914)

«Y ordenó a Hermes, guía de los hombres, poner en ella una mente desvergonzada y un carácter engañoso. Y el argicida obedeció al soberano Zeus Cronida e implantó en ella mentiras, palabras engañosas y el carácter de una ladrona.»

— Works and Days, vv. 67–68 · Hesiod, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914)

“But the woman took off the great lid of the jar with her hands and scattered all these and her thought caused sorrow and mischief to men. Only Hope remained there in an unbreakable home within under the rim of the great jar, and did not fly out at the door; for ere that, the lid of the jar stopped her, by the will of Aegis-holding Zeus who gathers the clouds.”

— Works and Days, vv. 94–98 · Hesiod, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914)
Philological note: The original term is pithos (πίθος) — a large storage jar, buried in the ground, used to store oil, wine, and grain. It is not a box. The confusion with pyxis (πυξίς, “small box”) was introduced five centuries later by Erasmus of Rotterdam.
Edition: Hesiod, Works and Days, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1914).
c. 700 BC
Hesíodo
Theogony · Theogonia
+
Context: The first written mention of the name Pandora. The Theogony version is briefer and does not mention the pithos — Pandora appears as a direct punishment, not as a bearer of gifts.

“He bade famous Hephaestus make haste and mix earth with water and to put in it the voice and strength of human kind, and fashion a sweet lovely maiden-shape, like to the immortal goddesses in face.”

— Theogony, vv. 571–575 · Hesiod, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914)

“And he called this woman Pandora — All-Endowed — because all they who dwelt on Olympus gave each a gift, a plague to men who eat bread.”

— Theogony, v. 585 · Hesiod, trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914)
Note: “Pandora” (Πανδώρα) in Greek means literally “she who has all gifts” or “she who gives all gifts”. Hesiod uses it ironically: the one who received all the gods’ gifts — as a weapon against men. Modern scholars debate whether the name originally meant “the all-giver” (the gods create her as a gift) or “the all-endowed” (she who receives the gifts).
c. 470 BC
Esquilo
Prometheus Bound · Prometheus Desmotes
+
Context: A Greek tragedy narrating Prometheus’s punishment from his own perspective. The dramatic basis for Peter Paul Rubens’s painting and the archetype of the rebel who suffers for his audacity.

“I caused mortals no longer to foresee their doom. I placed in them blind hopes… And besides this, I gave them fire… With it they shall learn many crafts.”

— Prometheus Bound, vv. 250–254 · Aeschylus, trans. Herbert Weir Smyth (1926)

“Zeus nailed me here in these unbreakable fetters. The eagle that Zeus has sent… comes to rend my immortal liver with his crooked talons. Each night it grows again, and each day the cursed bird returns to his feast.”

— Prometheus Bound, vv. 1017–1025 · Aeschylus, trans. Herbert Weir Smyth (1926)
Connection: Rubens’s Prometheus Bound (c. 1612, Philadelphia Museum of Art) is the final painting in the episode — the closing of the cycle. Prometheus suffers the consequences of the act that Pandora embodies.
c. 380 BC
Platón
Protágoras · 321c–322a
+
Context: In this dialogue, the sophist Protagoras narrates a different version of the Prometheus myth with no direct mention of Pandora. It shows that the myth had multiple versions in classical Greece and was not a fixed narrative.

“Prometheus, not knowing how he could devise his salvation, stole from Hephaestus and Athene the gift of skill in the arts, together with fire — for without fire the gift of the arts is useless — and gave it to man.”

— Plato, Protagoras, 321c–322a · trans. Benjamin Jowett (1871)
Significance: In Plato’s version, fire is a metaphor for techné (technical and artisanal skill). The emphasis is on what makes man civilised — not on punishment through woman, as in Hesiod.

“Only Hope remained there within,
and did not fly out at the door.”

Hesiod · Works and Days · c. 700 BC · trans. H.G. Evelyn-White (1914)
1508 AD
Erasmo de Rotterdam
Adagia · «Ex Pyxide Pandorae»
+
Context: The Dutch humanist published his collection of Greek and Latin proverbs. When translating Hesiod’s pithos passage, he used the Latin term pyxis — a small box, jewellery case, or pill container. The error became canonical for five hundred years.
The error: Pithos (πίθος) is an enormous jar, up to a metre and a half tall, buried in the ground for storing oil, wine, and grain. Pyxis (πυξίς) is a small box. Erasmus confuses two entirely different Greek terms — or perhaps chose deliberately to make the story more visual and intimate in the Christian tradition (like a coffer of sins).

«Ex Pyxide Pandorae» · “From Pandora’s box” — as if to say: from a mysterious and inexhaustible source of evils.

— Erasmus, Adagia, II.4.17 (1508) · First use of ‘pyxis’ (box) in place of Hesiod’s ‘pithos’ (jar)
Fuente académica: Dora Panofsky & Erwin Panofsky, Pandora's Box: The Changing Aspects of a Mythical Symbol (Princeton University Press, 1956). Estudio definitivo sobre la transformación del mito.
Consequence: All Western iconography from the Renaissance to the present shows Pandora with a box or chest. The original jar disappears from the collective imagination. Magritte painted a box in 1951. Children’s books show a box. The phrase “to open Pandora’s box” carries Erasmus’s error in every utterance.
1618 AD
Michael Maier
Atalanta Fugiens · Frontispiece
+
Context: The crowning work of Renaissance alchemy. The German physician Michael Maier encoded alchemical knowledge in 50 engraved emblems, each with a three-voice musical fugue. The image used in the episode is the frontispiece (engraved title page) of the 1618 edition — showing Atalanta, Hippomenes, Hercules, Venus, and the allegorical figures of the race, surrounding the work’s title.

“In the Renaissance alchemical tradition, Pandora represented the prima materia: the original vessel containing all elements before the Great Work. The jar is not punishment — it is origin.”

— Production note · Crónicas Eternas, 2026
Production note: The image is the full frontispiece of Atalanta Fugiens, published in Oppenheim by Hieronymi Galleri (1618). It includes the figures of classical mythology and the full title: «Emblemata Nova de Secretis Naturæ Chymica». Public domain.
1885–86 AD
George Frederic Watts
Letter to Madeline Wyndham · 8 Dec. 1885
+
Context: Watts described his painting Hope in a personal letter before its completion. It is the only document in which the painter directly explains his intention. The painting (1886) is exhibited at Tate Britain, London.

“I am painting a picture of Hope sitting on a globe, with her eyes bandaged, touching a lyre that has only one string left.”

— G.F. Watts, letter to Madeline Wyndham, 8 December 1885
Relevance: The letter confirms the painter’s intention: the blindfold is deliberate (hope is blind), the broken lyre is deliberate (only one string remains), the hunched posture is deliberate. This is not a painting about faith — it is about the fragility of pressing on with what remains.
Note: A contemporary critic suggested the painting nearly bore the title Despair. Watts did not write this — but the remark reflects the ambiguity the painter consciously sought.
1878 AD
Friedrich Nietzsche
Human, All Too Human · §71
+
Context: In his first book of aphorisms, Nietzsche reinterprets the Pandora myth from a radically anti-optimistic perspective. Elpis (Hope) is not the consolation that saves humanity — it is the last and most dangerous evil.

“Zeus wanted man, however tortured he might be by the other evils, not to fling away his life, but to go on letting himself be tormented again and again. For this purpose he gives man hope: in truth it is the most evil of evils because it prolongs man’s torment.”

— Nietzsche, Human, All Too Human, §71 (1878) · trans. R.J. Hollingdale (1986)
Connection to Hesiod: Nietzsche reads something that Hesiod left ambiguous: why does Hope remain trapped in the pithos? To protect men from it, or so they cannot escape its effect? If hope is a good, it should have been released. If it remained enclosed, perhaps it is one more evil.
