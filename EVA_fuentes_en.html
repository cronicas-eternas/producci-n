c. 900–500 BC
Genesis 2–3
Hebrew Bible / Old Testament · Canonical source
+
Context: The creation of Eve appears in two distinct versions within Genesis. Genesis 1 (attributed to the Priestly source, 6th century BC) creates man and woman simultaneously. Genesis 2–3 (the Yahwist source, 10th–9th century BC) narrates the creation of Eve from Adam’s rib — the version the episode develops.

“And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman, and brought her unto the man. And Adam said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.”

— Genesis 2:22–23 · King James Version (1611)

“Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?”

— Genesis 3:1 · King James Version (1611)

“And the LORD God said unto the serpent, Because thou hast done this, thou art cursed above all cattle, and above every beast of the field… Unto the woman he said, I will greatly multiply thy sorrow and thy conception; in sorrow thou shalt bring forth children; and thy desire shall be to thy husband, and he shall rule over thee.”

— Genesis 3:1 · King James Version (1611)4–16
Critical note: Two editorial traditions exist within Genesis. In Genesis 1:27, man and woman are created simultaneously (“male and female he created them”). In Genesis 2, Eve is created from Adam’s rib. The coexistence of both versions is the basis for the later development of the Lilith myth as the “first woman” prior to Eve.
Edition: King James Version (1611) · Authorised Version for all quotations.
c. 180 BC
Jesus Ben Sira
Eclesiástico (Siracida) · 25:24
+
Context: Ecclesiasticus is a deuterocanonical text (included in the Catholic Bible but not in the Jewish or Protestant canon). This is the first explicit interpretation of Eve as the origin of sin — predating the Pauline texts.

“Of the woman came the beginning of sin, and through her we all die.”

— Eclesiástico (Siracida) 25:24
Significance: Ben Sira reads Genesis through a misogynistic lens that the original text does not contain explicitly. This line, together with the Pauline text of 1 Timothy 2, establishes the theological tradition blaming Eve for human death. Genesis itself does not say this: it says that the man also ate, that both are expelled, and that both receive specific curses.
c. AD 50–60
Paul of Tarsus (attrib.)
First Epistle to Timothy · 2:11–15
+
Context: The First Epistle to Timothy is one of the most influential texts in the theological construction of Eve as the primary agent of the Fall. Most contemporary biblical scholars consider it pseudepigraphical (written in Paul’s name, not by him).

“Let the woman learn in silence with all subjection… For Adam was first formed, then Eve. And Adam was not deceived, but the woman being deceived was in the transgression.”

— 1 Timothy 2:11–14 · King James Version (1611)
Critical note: This text introduces the hierarchy of creation (Adam first = greater authority) and the deception argument (Eve was deceived → she is more susceptible to error). These are the two pillars of the patriarchal theology of Eve that would dominate Western thought for fifteen centuries.
Academic status: Most current scholars consider 1 Timothy deutero-Pauline, written between AD 80–100 (cf. Bart Ehrman, Forged, HarperOne, 2011).

“Adam was not deceived,
but the woman being deceived
was in the transgression.”

1 Timothy 2:14 · KJV · c. AD 80–100 · The text that defined Eve for fifteen centuries
c. AD 100–300
Textos de Nag Hammadi
Apocryphon of John · Eve as Epinoia (First Thought)
+
Context: In 1945, a library of 52 Gnostic texts in Coptic was discovered at Nag Hammadi, Egypt. The Apocryphon of John contains the most radical inversion of the Genesis myth: Eve is not responsible for the Fall — she is the bearer of divine knowledge who descends into the material world to awaken Adam.

«Epínoia de la luz fue escondida dentro de Adán, para que los arcontes no pudieran alcanzarla... Y la llamaron Eva, "la que da vida", porque ella es la madre de todos los vivientes.»

— Apocryphon of John (NH II,1) · Nag Hammadi Codex II · trans. Frederick Wisse (1977)

“The Demiurge did not want them to be equal. So he took Eve and separated her from Adam. But the Epinoia entered Eve in secret, and Adam recognised his likeness.”

— Apocryphon of John (NH II,1) · trans. Frederick Wisse (1977)
Gnostic inversion: In this cosmology, the Demiurge (the God of Genesis) is not the true God but an ignorant being who creates the material world as a prison. Eve / Epinoia descends to free Adam from the Demiurge’s deception. The serpent is the instrument of Higher Wisdom, not of evil.
Edition: James M. Robinson (ed.), The Nag Hammadi Library (Brill, 1978). Translation: Frederick Wisse, in Robinson (1977/1988).
c. AD 100–350
Textos de Nag Hammadi
Testimony of Truth (NH IX,3) · The serpent as teacher
+
Context: The Testimony of Truth contains the most complete inversion of the myth: the serpent is the teacher of knowledge, and the God of Genesis acts as a jealous tyrant who fears losing control of his creatures.

«¿Qué tipo de Dios es este? Primero envidió a Adán porque comió del árbol del conocimiento. Segundo, dijo: "Adán, ¿dónde estás?" Dios no tiene presciencia. Y la serpiente era más sabia que todos los dioses.»

— Testimony of Truth (NH IX,3) · Nag Hammadi Codex IX · trans. Søren Giversen & Birger A. Pearson (1977)
Relevance: This text is the argument that most troubled the Church Fathers: if God is omniscient, why does he ask where Adam is? The Testimony of Truth uses Genesis’s own internal logic to question the nature of the God who narrates it.
c. AD 700–1000
Anónimo
Alphabet of Ben Sira · The first woman before Eve
+
Context: The Alphabet of Ben Sira (Jewish medieval, c. AD 700–1000) is the first text to name Lilith as Adam’s first wife, created simultaneously from the earth, who refuses to lie beneath him and leaves Eden. Her absence leaves space for Eve.

“When the Holy One, blessed be He, created the first man, He decided it was not good for him to be alone, and created a woman for him from the earth, and called her Lilith. They immediately quarrelled. She said: I will not lie below you. He said: I will not lie below you but above you. Each wished to be dominant.”

— Alphabet of Ben Sira · c. 9th century AD · trans. Norman Bronznick (1981)
Origin of the myth: Lilith does not appear as a character in Genesis — her name appears only once, in the book of Isaiah (34:14), as a creature of the desert. The myth of Lilith as Adam’s first wife is a medieval creation that resolves the contradiction between Genesis 1 and Genesis 2.
Academic source: Raphael Patai, The Hebrew Goddess (Wayne State University Press, 1967). The reference study on the Lilith tradition.
c. AD 1280
Moses de León (attrib.)
Zohar · Book of Splendour · Kabbalah
+
Context: The Zohar is the fundamental text of Jewish Kabbalah, probably written in thirteenth-century Spain and attributed to Rabbi Simeon bar Yochai (2nd century AD). It extensively develops the feminine duality of Lilith vs. the Shekhinah as the feminine aspect of divinity.

“At the beginning of the world there was a female on Adam’s left side, hardened in evil… called Lilith. This woman assumed the role of Samael.”

— Zohar I, 34b · trans. Harry Sperling & Maurice Simon (1984)

“Lilith’s chief desire is to destroy the children of the world. And therefore Eve exists — so that there may be a feminine principle bound to love, not to hatred.”

— Zohar III, 19a · trans. Harry Sperling & Maurice Simon (1984)
Relevance for the episode: The Kabbalistic tradition generates the Lilith/Eve duality as the dark feminine principle vs. the luminous feminine principle — both necessary, neither complete without the other. This is the root of the modern Gnostic interpretation of Eve as a bearer of embodied divine wisdom.
1945 AD
Carl Gustav Jung
Aurora Consurgens · Edition and commentary
+
Context: Jung edited and commented on the medieval manuscript Aurora Consurgens as part of his work Mysterium Coniunctionis. For Jung, the feminine figure that emerges in alchemical and Gnostic texts (Sophia, Epinoia, Aurora) is the projection of the anima — the unconscious feminine principle of the collective masculine psyche.

“Eve is not merely a historical or theological figure — she is an archetype. The name given in every culture to the feminine principle that awakens masculine consciousness: Sophia among the Gnostics, Prajna in Buddhism, Shakti in Hinduism.”

— Jung, Mysterium Coniunctionis, CW vol. 14 (1956) · trans. R.F.C. Hull (1963)
Source: C.G. Jung, Mysterium Coniunctionis, Collected Works vol. 14 (Routledge, 1963). Marie-Louise von Franz, Aurora Consurgens (1966).
